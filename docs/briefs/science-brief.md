# World science brief: a volcanic moon in atmospheric transition

*Source material Hunter shared on 2026-10-14, written outside this repo. It's kept
verbatim apart from removed citation markup; the references are listed at the end. Not
canon. How it fits with the shield arc is in [the lore research](../world/lore-research.md).*

**Project:** Barren Moon Survival Game
**Document type:** Scientific worldbuilding, environmental lore, and simulation requirements
**Design priorities:** Scientific plausibility, predictable environmental hazards, geological storytelling, and survival without combat

## 1. Executive summary

### The premise

The player is the sole survivor on a barren, geologically unstable moon orbiting a gas giant. The moon has a substantial atmosphere, powerful prevailing winds, vast fields of mineral dust, and periodic dust storms capable of overwhelming exposed equipment and making travel dangerous.

The moon was not always this way. Its present landscape is the result of a long geological transition. A change in its orbital dynamics altered the distribution of tidal heating inside the moon. This accelerated geological activity, fractured the crust, mobilized volatile compounds, and exposed large quantities of weatherable surface material. Over time, erosion, atmospheric chemistry, and volatile loss transformed a more varied landscape into an arid, dust-dominated environment.

The player arrives near the end of this transition, when the landscape is largely barren but the climate has not yet settled into a stable regime.

The environment is not random. Prevailing winds, local terrain, temperature cycles, and recurring atmospheric patterns determine when storms develop and where they travel. The player can learn these patterns and make informed decisions about when to travel, where to seek shelter, and how much risk to accept for better supplies.

### The scientific model

The setting combines five established physical mechanisms:

1. **Orbital resonance and tidal heating** supply internal geological energy.
2. **Volcanism and crustal fractures** release gases and expose fresh material.
3. **Mechanical and chemical weathering** produce loose mineral grains and fine dust.
4. **Atmospheric circulation and temperature differences** generate winds.
5. **Aeolian transport** lifts, suspends, and redistributes dust across the surface.

Atmospheric escape and the depletion of surface volatiles provide a long-term direction for the moon's evolution.

These mechanisms are individually plausible. Their exact combination, timescales, and severity are speculative and should be treated as deliberate fictional extrapolation.

## 2. The astronomical setting

### 2.1 The host planet

Place the moon in orbit around a large gas giant, broadly analogous to Jupiter or Saturn.

The gas giant provides several useful environmental and narrative features:

- Strong gravitational tides acting on the moon.
- A system of neighboring moons capable of influencing its orbit.
- Periodic eclipses, during which the gas giant blocks direct sunlight.
- A visually dominant planet that creates an unmistakable sense of scale.
- A long orbital history capable of shaping the moon's geological evolution.

The giant planet does not need to be unusually exotic. The interesting physics occurs through the interaction of the moon, its orbit, its interior, and its atmosphere.

### 2.2 The moon's size

**Recommended design choice: a large moon, approximately Titan-sized or somewhat smaller.**

Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a radius of approximately 2,575 km and a substantial nitrogen-rich atmosphere. It demonstrates that a moon can retain a dense atmosphere and support atmospheric circulation, surface liquids, dunes, and dust transport.

A large moon is advantageous because its gravity makes long-term atmospheric retention more plausible than it would be on a small asteroid-like body.

The final atmosphere also depends on temperature, atmospheric composition, escape processes, and the moon's history. Size alone does not guarantee that an atmosphere will survive.

A smaller moon can still work, but maintaining a thick atmosphere over geological timescales would require more careful justification.

### 2.3 Orbital configuration

The moon orbits within a system of neighboring satellites. Its orbit is initially maintained in, or influenced by, a gravitational resonance.

An orbital resonance occurs when the orbital periods of two or more bodies have a ratio close to a ratio of small integers. Repeated gravitational interactions can then reinforce particular orbital variations.

Jupiter's Io, Europa, and Ganymede provide a real example. Their orbital relationships help maintain Io's eccentric orbit, enabling Jupiter's gravity to continually deform Io and generate internal heat.

For the fictional moon, the important consequence is not the resonance itself. It is the effect that changing orbital conditions can have on tidal heating.

## 3. The initiating event: why the moon begins to change

### 3.1 Recommended trigger: a change in orbital resonance

The strongest natural trigger is a change in the moon system's orbital configuration.

Over a long period, tidal interactions cause moons to exchange angular momentum with their host planet and with one another. Their orbits can migrate. A resonance may strengthen, weaken, or be crossed as orbital relationships evolve.

In the fictional system, the moon experiences a change in its orbital eccentricity or in the amplitude of its tidal deformation. This increases or redistributes tidal heating within the moon.

The result is a geological transition rather than a single catastrophic event.

**Important distinction:** orbital changes do not directly manufacture an atmosphere or generate surface wind. They can change the moon's internal heat budget, which can affect volcanism, crustal deformation, and the release of gases already present in the interior.

### 3.2 Why this works better than a single enormous impact

A giant impact could fracture the crust, excavate fresh material, and release volatiles. However, a single impact large enough to produce the entire environmental transition would introduce many additional questions about the moon's survival, debris, and orbital stability.

A gradual change in tidal heating provides a more flexible explanation:

- It can affect multiple geological regions.
- It can produce recurring volcanic and tectonic activity.
- It can expose fresh material over a broad area.
- It can sustain a long environmental transition.
- It does not require an improbably convenient catastrophe immediately before the game begins.

A large impact can remain part of the moon's earlier history, but it is not necessary as the principal trigger.

### 3.3 How sudden should the change be?

The orbital evolution should generally be gradual on human timescales. Resonance transitions and major changes in tidal heating are not inherently instantaneous.

For the game, distinguish three timescales:

| Process | Suggested fictional timescale | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital evolution | Thousands to millions of years | Long-term geological cause |
| Surface transformation | Thousands to hundreds of thousands of years | Development of widespread dust deposits |
| Local geological events | Hours to decades | Fresh fractures, eruptions, and newly exposed deposits |
| Weather and dust storms | Minutes to days | Immediate survival gameplay |

These are worldbuilding ranges, not predictions for a particular moon.

The player experiences the fast processes. The long processes explain why the world exists in its current state.

## 4. Geological transformation: from a varied landscape to a barren moon

### 4.1 What the moon looked like before

The moon should not begin as an Earth-like planet covered in forests and oceans. A more plausible starting point is a cold, rocky or icy satellite with a varied surface.

Possible original features include:

- Large exposed rock formations.
- Ancient impact basins.
- Deposits of water ice or other frozen volatiles.
- Fractured crust surrounding older volcanic regions.
- Sediments accumulated in depressions.
- Regions containing frozen gases or chemically altered surface material.

The surface may have been relatively stable for long intervals, interrupted by periodic geological activity.

### 4.2 Tidal heating destabilizes the crust

Changing tidal stresses and increased internal heating can contribute to fracturing, melting at depth, and volcanic activity.

Io is the clearest Solar System example of extreme tidal heating. Its interior is heated by repeated gravitational deformation, supporting intense volcanism and continual resurfacing.

The fictional moon does not need Io's extreme volcanic activity. In fact, a less extreme, more distributed form of activity better supports a landscape that remains traversable.

Possible consequences include:

- New fissures and faults.
- Volcanic deposits spread across old terrain.
- Subsurface reservoirs becoming connected to the surface.
- Fractured rock becoming easier to disaggregate.
- Previously buried deposits being exposed by uplift or collapse.

The surface becomes a source of fresh material rather than a static layer of ancient rock.

### 4.3 Where the dust comes from

Dust is not a substance that simply appears because a landscape becomes a desert. It must be produced from existing material or deposited from elsewhere.

On Earth, rocks are broken down through mechanical processes such as impacts, thermal stress, and abrasion, as well as chemical weathering. Water, ice, and biological activity are important contributors to many terrestrial landscapes, but a barren moon can rely more heavily on mechanical processes.

For this moon, use several sources of loose material.

**Thermal fracturing:** repeated heating and cooling cause different minerals to expand and contract at different rates, encouraging cracks and grain separation.

**Impact gardening:** micrometeorite impacts repeatedly fracture, pulverize, and redistribute the upper surface.

**Volcanic fragmentation:** explosive or gas-driven eruptions can produce fine particles, while ash and fragmented deposits provide additional material for later erosion.

**Frost and volatile cycling:** gases that freeze onto the surface and later sublime can disrupt porous deposits. Repeated deposition and removal may expose or loosen grains.

**Wind abrasion:** particles already being transported by the wind strike exposed rock and gradually wear it down.

**Slope failure and erosion:** fractured terrain produces scree, sediment fans, and loose deposits that can be transported into basins.

Together, these processes generate a continuing supply of dust and sand.

### 4.4 Why the surface becomes desert-like

A desert-like environment emerges when loose surface material is abundant and the processes that remove, transport, or consolidate it do not keep pace with its production.

On the fictional moon:

1. Geological activity fractures the crust and exposes fresh material.
2. Weathering and impacts generate loose grains.
3. Wind transports the finest particles over large distances.
4. Coarser grains accumulate into dunes, ripples, and sediment fields.
5. Fine dust settles in sheltered basins and is repeatedly remobilized.
6. Long-term depletion of surface volatiles reduces the likelihood of persistent liquid-based sediment transport.

The resulting landscape can be dominated by dust plains, eroded ridges, dunes, and exposed bedrock.

**Scientific caveat:** erosion does not automatically turn an entire moon into a desert. The outcome depends on its composition, temperature, volatile inventory, atmosphere, gravity, and duration of exposure. The setting should establish that this particular moon has a large supply of erodible material and relatively little persistent liquid capable of stabilizing it.

## 5. Atmospheric evolution: where the air comes from

This is the most important constraint in the worldbuilding.

A substantial atmosphere cannot be explained merely by saying that the moon has become more volcanic. Volcanism can release gases, but the total atmosphere depends on the amount of gas available, how quickly it is replenished, and how quickly it is lost to space or trapped on the surface.

### 5.1 Start with an existing atmosphere

The most plausible starting point is that the moon already possesses an atmosphere, inherited from its formation and maintained by a combination of internal reservoirs and geological activity.

Its original atmosphere may have been thicker or thinner than the atmosphere it has today. The transition changes its composition, circulation, and surface interactions.

This avoids requiring a previously airless moon to suddenly acquire an enormous atmosphere.

### 5.2 The role of volcanic outgassing

Volcanic activity releases gases dissolved in magma or stored in subsurface reservoirs.

Potential constituents include:

- Water vapor.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Sulfur dioxide and other sulfur-bearing gases.
- Nitrogen-bearing compounds.
- Other volatile species appropriate to the moon's composition.

The actual mixture depends on the interior chemistry and temperature.

Io demonstrates how volcanic activity can sustain a thin sulfur-dioxide atmosphere. It also demonstrates that atmospheric pressure can vary as gases freeze onto the surface during eclipses and return to the atmosphere when warmed by sunlight.

However, Io is not a suitable direct model for a dense, breathable atmosphere or a conventional desert climate. Its atmosphere is extremely tenuous.

For this setting, volcanic outgassing should replenish and modify an atmosphere that already has a substantial reservoir of long-lived gases.

### 5.3 The preferred atmospheric composition

**Recommended fictional atmosphere: predominantly nitrogen, with carbon dioxide and smaller amounts of sulfur-bearing and other volcanic gases.**

Nitrogen provides a relatively unreactive bulk gas. Carbon dioxide and trace gases can contribute to radiative heating and atmospheric chemistry. Sulfur-bearing compounds can help distinguish the moon's volcanic regions through color, haze, and surface deposits.

This composition is an extrapolation from known planetary chemistry, not a claim that a volcanic moon would naturally acquire this exact mixture.

The atmosphere should be dense enough for meaningful aerodynamic drag and sustained circulation, but its final pressure should be selected alongside the moon's gravity, temperature, and atmospheric retention history.

A breathable atmosphere is not necessary. Indeed, a toxic atmosphere makes sealed shelters and environmental protection central to survival.

### 5.4 Why the atmosphere does not disappear immediately

Atmospheric escape is a gradual process influenced by gravity, temperature, atmospheric composition, ultraviolet radiation, charged particles, and other factors.

A moon around a gas giant may also experience interactions with the planet's magnetosphere, which can alter atmospheric chemistry and contribute to atmospheric loss.

The world should not rely on a thin atmosphere remaining unchanged indefinitely. Instead, establish a balance between:

- A pre-existing atmospheric inventory.
- Continuing volcanic replenishment.
- Surface and subsurface storage of volatile compounds.
- Condensation and sublimation.
- Chemical reactions that create or destroy atmospheric constituents.
- Escape to space.

The atmosphere can be declining overall while remaining substantial on the timescale of the game.

### 5.5 The critical distinction: gas release versus atmospheric retention

Releasing gas from the crust does not guarantee that it becomes a permanent atmosphere. Gas may condense, react with surface materials, escape, or become trapped again.

For the setting, the geological transition should temporarily increase gas release while the moon retains enough of its original atmosphere to sustain weather.

The long-term trend can still be atmospheric decline.

This creates an important narrative tension: the moon's atmosphere is not necessarily getting stronger just because volcanic activity is increasing.

## 6. Wind: how the moon produces predictable weather

### 6.1 An atmosphere is necessary, but not sufficient

Wind is the movement of gas driven by pressure gradients. These gradients arise from differences in heating, temperature, density, and atmospheric circulation.

A moon does not need to be a planet to have wind. It does need a sufficiently substantial atmosphere.

Titan is a particularly relevant analogue because its atmospheric circulation is influenced by seasonal heating, its slow rotation, and the distribution of solar energy. NASA's discussion of Titan's winds describes large-scale atmospheric circulation and substantial variations in wind speed with altitude.

### 6.2 What drives the wind on this moon?

Use three interacting mechanisms.

**Solar heating:** the sunlit side and the nightside develop temperature differences. The atmosphere redistributes energy, producing circulation.

**Seasonal heating:** changes in illumination alter the location and strength of large-scale circulation patterns. For a moon orbiting a gas giant, the giant planet's orbital period around its star and its axial tilt influence the moon's long seasonal cycle.

**Topographic effects:** mountains, crater rims, ridges, and basins channel or redirect wind. Narrow passages may accelerate airflow, while sheltered valleys may experience much weaker winds.

The resulting wind field has a predictable regional structure, with local variations caused by terrain.

### 6.3 Can the wind become strong enough for massive dust storms?

Yes, in principle, but the details matter.

Titan provides direct evidence of a substantial atmosphere, extensive dune fields, and dust storms. In a 2018 study, researchers interpreted transient atmospheric features observed by Cassini as dust storms over Titan's equatorial dune regions. The inferred near-surface winds required were much stronger than typical winds estimated from the Huygens landing measurements.

That evidence supports the existence of dust transport on an atmospheric moon. It does not mean that Titan regularly experiences planet-wide, catastrophic sandstorms.

For the game, extrapolate from this evidence by giving the fictional moon:

- A large supply of fine, loose particles.
- An atmosphere dense enough to transfer momentum to those particles.
- Strong regional pressure gradients.
- Seasonal or recurring conditions that generate gusts.
- Terrain that funnels and intensifies local airflow.

The atmosphere's density, surface gravity, grain sizes, and threshold wind speeds should be designed together.

Do not assume that a thin atmosphere will automatically produce powerful dust storms simply because the landscape is dusty.

### 6.4 Why the storms are predictable

The game should model weather as a changing but learnable system.

Use a prevailing wind direction that changes according to a recurring atmospheric cycle. Superimpose local gusts, terrain-driven variations, and occasional intense storm events.

The storm's progression can follow recognizable stages:

1. **Precursor conditions:** changes in wind direction, temperature, pressure, or airborne dust.
2. **Dust mobilization:** exposed particles begin moving across the surface.
3. **Storm growth:** winds strengthen and the dust plume expands.
4. **Peak conditions:** visibility falls, movement becomes hazardous, and exposed equipment takes damage.
5. **Decay:** winds weaken and dust settles into new deposits.

The exact cycle can be fictional, but it should remain internally consistent.

The player should learn to infer storm development from environmental signals rather than depend on a random timer.

## 7. Why the moon is transitioning toward a barren state

The geological transition and atmospheric evolution should be related, but they should not be treated as the same process.

### Phase A: A previously varied surface

The moon has an ancient crust containing fractured rock, frozen volatiles, and deposits accumulated over geological time. Its atmosphere is already present.

### Phase B: Orbital evolution

Long-term changes in the moon system alter the moon's orbital eccentricity and tidal deformation. Internal heating increases or shifts geographically.

### Phase C: Geological activation

Fractures propagate, volcanic activity increases, and previously buried materials become exposed. Volcanic gases enter the atmosphere, while fresh surface deposits provide new sources of loose material.

### Phase D: Accelerated surface erosion

Thermal fracturing, impacts, and wind abrasion generate and redistribute fine particles. Exposed volatile deposits undergo repeated freezing, sublimation, and redistribution.

### Phase E: Atmospheric and climatic reorganization

Outgassing modifies the atmospheric composition, while seasonal heating and the changing surface influence circulation. Regional winds mobilize large dust deposits.

### Phase F: The new environmental regime

Much of the moon is now arid, dust-dominated, and heavily eroded. Volcanic activity continues in localized regions, but the landscape is no longer dominated by the original surface materials.

Atmospheric escape and depletion of accessible volatiles continue over longer timescales.

### Why the transition is not necessarily permanent

The moon may eventually reach a new quasi-steady state, in which geological production, surface transport, atmospheric replenishment, and atmospheric loss fluctuate around a relatively stable long-term regime.

The present climate need not be a permanent end state. It can be a relatively long-lived interval in a continuing geological evolution.

This gives the world a natural history without requiring it to be changing dramatically every day.

## 8. Alternative mechanisms and their tradeoffs

### Option A: Orbital resonance transition

**Recommended as the primary historical trigger.**

Advantages:
- Uses established celestial mechanics.
- Provides a credible source of changing internal heat.
- Connects the moon's geological history to its gas giant system.
- Supports a gradual, distributed transformation.

Limitations:
- Orbital evolution does not directly generate an atmosphere.
- The precise resonance history must be constructed consistently.
- Geological consequences unfold on timescales much longer than a typical game session.

### Option B: A major impact

Advantages:
- Can excavate fresh material.
- Can fracture the crust and release volatiles.
- Provides a dramatic historical event.

Limitations:
- A sufficiently large impact may cause extensive melting, ejecta, and orbital consequences.
- It does not automatically create a stable atmosphere.
- It is less effective as an explanation for sustained geological activity unless other mechanisms are involved.

### Option C: A volatile-rich interior

Advantages:
- Provides a reservoir for atmospheric replenishment.
- Supports outgassing, frost deposits, and repeated volatile cycling.
- Helps explain why the atmosphere remains substantial.

Limitations:
- A volatile reservoir alone does not explain powerful wind.
- The composition and accessibility of the reservoir need to be specified.
- Outgassing must be balanced against atmospheric loss and surface trapping.

### Preferred combination

Use **Option A to initiate the geological transition, Option C to sustain the atmospheric inventory, and the moon's pre-existing atmosphere plus solar-driven circulation to produce the weather**.

A major impact can be part of the moon's ancient history, but it should not be required to explain every environmental feature.

## 9. Environmental storytelling

The player should be able to infer the moon's history from its surface.

### Geological evidence

- Fractured terrain surrounding old volcanic deposits.
- Layers of ash or fragmented rock partially buried by younger dust.
- Exposed mineral veins where the crust has split.
- Collapsed depressions and fissures.
- Ancient surfaces preserved beneath more recent deposits.
- Regions where erosion has removed fine material and exposed bedrock.

### Atmospheric evidence

- Frost or volatile deposits in sheltered regions.
- Discolored rock near volcanic vents.
- Local haze or unusual sky coloration.
- Different weather behavior between exposed plains and protected basins.
- Persistent dust deposits along prevailing wind corridors.

### Evidence of changing climate

- Old landforms that indicate a previous pattern of sediment transport.
- Recently exposed deposits that have not yet been fully redistributed.
- Partially buried structures or geological features.
- Dust accumulation patterns inconsistent with the present dominant wind direction.
- Localized deposits of material that appear to have been transported from distant regions.

These features should imply a history without spelling out the entire explanation in exposition.

The moon should feel like a physical system whose current state can be understood through observation.

## 10. Gameplay implications

The science should support the survival loop rather than become a separate simulation that does not affect the player.

### 10.1 Movement

Movement consumes stamina and time. The player travels through a landscape with different exposure levels, wind conditions, and access to shelter.

Open plains offer faster traversal but greater exposure. Valleys and rock formations may reduce wind intensity but can create difficult terrain or limited escape routes.

### 10.2 Shelter

Shelter reduces exposure to wind and airborne particles. It should not necessarily eliminate every environmental hazard.

Possible shelter characteristics include:

- Wind protection.
- Structural integrity.
- Sealing against fine dust.
- Thermal insulation.
- Protection from airborne debris.
- Limited supplies or maintenance requirements.

The game should distinguish between sheltering from wind and surviving the atmosphere itself. If the atmosphere is toxic or unsuitable for breathing, the player needs life support regardless of whether a storm is occurring.

### 10.3 Hunger and resource consumption

Foraging, scavenging, and consuming supplies compete with travel and rest for the player's available time.

The storm system creates a meaningful decision: should the player continue toward a distant resource while conditions deteriorate, or turn back toward a safer location?

The storm should change the cost of a decision rather than arbitrarily invalidate it.

### 10.4 Equipment degradation

Fine dust can plausibly cause:

- Abrasion of exposed surfaces.
- Clogging of filters.
- Contamination of mechanical joints.
- Reduced visibility of optical sensors.
- Accumulation on solar panels or other exposed equipment.

The rate of degradation should depend on particle size, exposure duration, wind speed, and the equipment's design.

### 10.5 No combat

The environmental system should be the primary antagonist.

The player is threatened by exposure, navigation errors, exhaustion, limited supplies, equipment failure, and decisions made under uncertain conditions.

There is no need for hostile creatures or combat encounters to make the environment dangerous.

## 11. Recommended environmental simulation model

The initial prototype should use a deterministic or seeded environmental model rather than a computationally expensive atmospheric fluid simulation.

### Core environmental variables

| Variable | Meaning | Gameplay consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Wind direction | Dominant direction of atmospheric flow | Determines safer travel routes and exposed regions |
| Wind speed | Local airflow intensity | Influences movement, visibility, and equipment damage |
| Dust availability | Amount of loose material available for transport | Controls the potential severity of a dust event |
| Airborne dust concentration | Quantity of suspended particles | Controls visibility and exposure |
| Atmospheric pressure | Local surface pressure | Can influence wind force and particle transport |
| Temperature | Surface and near-surface thermal state | Influences equipment, thermal stress, and volatile cycling |
| Terrain exposure | How strongly the location experiences the prevailing wind | Modifies local hazard intensity |
| Shelter quality | Degree of protection provided by the player's shelter | Reduces environmental exposure |
| Storm phase | Developing, active, or decaying | Communicates the progression of the hazard |

### Suggested update model

1. Establish a recurring large-scale wind pattern.
2. Calculate regional wind intensity from the pattern, local temperature, and pressure conditions.
3. Modify the regional wind using terrain exposure and local sheltering.
4. Increase dust mobilization when wind stress exceeds a surface-dependent threshold.
5. Evolve airborne dust concentration as particles are entrained, transported, and deposited.
6. Derive visibility and equipment degradation from the resulting conditions.
7. Give the player readable precursor signals before the most dangerous conditions arrive.

A simplified dust transport model can use a threshold wind stress rather than simulating every grain.

The key is consistency: the same conditions should generally produce the same environmental response.

### Storm warning signals

Use multiple information channels:

- **Visual:** distant dust plumes, changing sky color, haze, and moving surface particles.
- **Audio:** increasing wind noise, impacts against exposed surfaces, and changes in the sound of the environment.
- **Equipment:** pressure readings, wind sensors, visibility estimates, and filter loading.
- **Terrain:** dust accumulation, scoured surfaces, and exposed wind corridors.
- **Inventory:** protective equipment and supplies that indicate how long the player can remain exposed.

The reliability of these signals can depend on the player's equipment and observational capability.

For example, a player with a functioning wind sensor may detect a change earlier than one relying on visual cues. This is a gameplay abstraction, not a claim that every storm can be forecast precisely.

## 12. Scientific limitations and guardrails

The development team should preserve the following distinctions.

**Do not claim that orbital changes directly create an atmosphere.** They can alter tidal heating and geological activity. Atmospheric replenishment requires a source of gas.

**Do not claim that volcanic outgassing automatically produces a dense nitrogen atmosphere.** Nitrogen must already exist in the interior, surface reservoirs, or original atmosphere.

**Do not assume that a moon can retain a thick atmosphere indefinitely.** Its mass, temperature, atmospheric composition, and escape history matter.

**Do not assume that more volcanic activity necessarily produces more dust storms.** Volcanism must generate accessible loose material, while the atmosphere must supply sufficient aerodynamic stress to mobilize it.

**Do not assume that Titan has constant hurricane-like surface winds.** Its atmosphere and dust storms are real, but the strongest storm conditions should be treated as intermittent rather than a permanent global state.

**Do not assume that a planet-like climate follows automatically from the presence of an atmosphere.** Atmospheric density, energy sources, surface properties, and circulation all matter.

**Do not treat a barren moon as a moon with no geological activity.** A surface can appear lifeless while tectonic, volcanic, and erosional processes remain active.

**Do not require the player to know the underlying astrophysics.** The simulation should remain consistent even when the player only observes the resulting weather.

## 13. Final recommended canon

The moon is a large, volatile-rich satellite orbiting a gas giant. It possesses a substantial nitrogen-rich atmosphere and an ancient crust containing rock, frozen compounds, and extensive fractured deposits.

Over geological time, orbital evolution alters its tidal heating. Geological activity increases in some regions, fracturing the crust and exposing fresh material. Volcanic outgassing replenishes and modifies the existing atmosphere, while impacts, thermal stress, and erosion create abundant loose particles.

Surface volatiles become less persistent across much of the landscape. Fine particles accumulate in exposed basins and plains, and recurring atmospheric circulation transports them across the surface.

The moon gradually becomes a dry, dust-dominated world. Atmospheric escape continues, but the moon retains enough of its original atmospheric inventory for wind and weather to remain active.

The player arrives during a period of severe dust activity. The climate is predictable in broad patterns, but local conditions can deteriorate quickly. The player's survival depends on learning the environment, managing supplies, maintaining shelter, and deciding when the potential reward of travel is worth the exposure.

The world does not require artificial atmosphere generators, ancient terraforming machines, or a contrived single catastrophe. Its defining danger emerges from the interaction of ordinary physical mechanisms operating under unusual planetary conditions.
